# Writing Well

Principles for clear prose, whoever's voice it is in. Use these when drafting and when cleaning up machine-written text. They are not corrections to apply to Danny's own drafts; see `SKILL.md` for that distinction.

## Know what you are saying

Most unclear writing comes from unclear thinking. Before drafting, state the main point in two sentences to someone who doesn't know the subject. If that is hard, the problem is the thinking, and polishing sentences will not fix it.

The same test works for a paragraph that is going badly: stop and ask what it is trying to say, then say that.

## Write for a specific reader

Decide who is reading and what they already know. The usual right target is someone intelligent and willing who was not there: they don't share your context, your abbreviations or your last three conversations.

- Lead with what the reader most needs. The first sentence should give them a reason to read the second.
- Define a term the first time it appears, then keep using that same term.
- Do not explain what the reader plainly knows, and do not skip a step they could not supply themselves.

## Say the thing

- **Start with the content.** Cut openings that announce a topic or describe what the piece is about to do. Begin with the first thing worth knowing.
- **Take a position** when the piece has one. Give the reasons. Mention the strongest objection if there is a real one.
- **Hedge once, where the doubt is.** State what is uncertain and why. Qualifiers on every sentence say nothing.
- **End when the content ends.** A closing paragraph that restates the piece can go.

## Choose plain words

- The ordinary word over the grand one: "use" not "utilise", "start" not "commence", "help" not "facilitate", "before" not "prior to", "because" not "due to the fact that".
- Verbs over nouns made from verbs: "we changed the process and the team got better", not "the implementation of the new process led to an improvement in team performance".
- The literal phrase over the figure of speech, unless the figure says something the literal version cannot.
- A longer or more technical word is right when it is more exact. "Interdependence" means something "linking" does not.

## Be concrete

Replace a general statement with the specific case wherever one exists.

- "Set up a weekly call where everyone shares what is blocking them", not "implement effective communication strategies".
- A number with its unit, a named person, a date, the actual error message.
- Name sources. "Experts suggest" and "many people find" tell the reader nothing they can check.

Specifics must be real. Never invent an example, a quotation, a statistic or a person to make a passage feel grounded. If the real detail is not available, say the general thing honestly or leave a marker for the author to fill in.

## Keep the reader with you

- One idea per paragraph, with the paragraphs in an order the reader could reconstruct.
- Connect sentences with the words that carry the logic: because, so, but, which means. Leaving them out makes the reader guess how one claim relates to the next.
- Keep one point of view and one tense unless there is a reason to shift.
- Let sentence length follow the content. A long sentence can hold a chain of reasoning; a short one can land a point. Prose where every sentence is the same length, or where length alternates on a pattern, is tiring to read.
- Read it aloud, or imagine doing so. Where you would stumble, the reader will too.

## Cut what the reader would not miss

Go through the draft and remove what does no work: a sentence that repeats the previous one, a qualifier that adds no information, a second example that makes the same point as the first, an adjective the noun already implies.

Cut for the reader, not to hit a length. Do not remove a step in the reasoning, a necessary definition, or the detail that makes a claim checkable.

## Punctuation serves the sentence

- A full stop is usually better than a semicolon.
- Brackets and dashes are for genuine asides. If a sentence has several, it is probably two sentences.
- A long sentence joined only by "and" with no commas is hard to follow. Split it, or punctuate it.
